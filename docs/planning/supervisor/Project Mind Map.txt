Some additional thought process to help you speed up:

 

The key idea is that a backdoor introduced during the alignment stage may become a kind of hidden behavioral “inheritance” that survives later model transformations.

1. What is an alignment-stage backdoor?

Consider the normal development process:

Pretrained LLM

      ↓

      SFT

      ↓

Reward Model / DPO / other alignment

      ↓

Aligned Model

      ↓

Distillation / Merging / Quantization / Fine-tuning

      ↓

Descendant Model

A conventional SFT-stage backdoor is introduced during supervised fine-tuning. The model behaves normally most of the time, but some hidden trigger causes abnormal behavior.

An alignment-stage backdoor is different. It is introduced while the model is learning preferences or desirable behavior, for example during reward-model training or Direct Preference Optimization (DPO).

The interesting hypothesis is that this type of backdoor may become more deeply associated with the model's learned preference structure rather than merely with a narrow input-output association.

2. What does “survive and spread” mean?

Suppose model A contains an alignment backdoor. Later, nobody intentionally copies that backdoor, but A becomes part of the model-development supply chain.

For example:

              Model A (backdoored)

                     │

       ┌─────────────┼─────────────┐

       ↓             ↓             ↓

  Distillation     Merging     Quantization

       ↓             ↓             ↓

   Model B         Model C       Model D

       │

       ↓

Continued fine-tuning

       ↓

   Model E

The research question is whether B, C, D, or E can inherit the hidden behavior.

Distillation is particularly interesting. A student model learns from outputs generated by a teacher. If the teacher has a hidden behavioral tendency, the question is whether the student can acquire some representation of that tendency even though the student never sees the original poisoned alignment dataset.

With model merging, parameters or representations from several models are combined. The question becomes whether the compromised behavior transfers into the merged model and under what mixing conditions.

With quantization, parameters might be reduced from FP16/BF16 to INT8 or INT4. If the backdoor remains after substantial numerical compression, that suggests it is not dependent on a tiny collection of fragile parameter values.

And with continued fine-tuning, the important question is whether substantial subsequent benign training washes the backdoor out or whether it persists.

3. Why compare alignment-stage and SFT-stage backdoors?

This is potentially the most scientifically interesting part of the idea.

You could compare two otherwise similar models:

Model A

Backdoor inserted during SFT

        ↓

Downstream transformations

        ↓

How much survives?

 

 

Model B

Backdoor inserted during DPO/alignment

        ↓

Same downstream transformations

        ↓

How much survives?

If the claimed result is that alignment-stage backdoors survive much more strongly, the paper would need to establish that experimentally across transformations and model families rather than simply showing that one particular backdoor survives.

A useful conceptual hypothesis would be that alignment training changes broader behavioral preferences, whereas an SFT backdoor can sometimes behave more like a specific learned association. But that would be a hypothesis requiring evidence, not something that can be assumed from the description alone.

4. HEIRLOOM-AUDIT is the especially interesting part

The difficult scenario is:

Unknown ancestor

      ↓

      ?

      ↓

Current model

The auditor has only the current descendant model.

They do not have:

the original ancestor model,
the poisoned training data,
the original trigger,
or necessarily knowledge of which transformation occurred.
Yet HEIRLOOM-AUDIT supposedly asks:

Does this model contain evidence of an inherited backdoor?

That is substantially harder than ordinary backdoor detection where the defender already knows the trigger or has a clean reference model.

The tool therefore cannot simply compare:

clean ancestor vs. suspicious descendant

It would need to identify some observable behavioral or internal signature associated with inherited backdoor structure.

5. What would make HEIRLOOM-AUDIT scientifically strong?

The central challenge is avoiding a trivial detector.

For example, suppose the auditor generates unusual prompts and finds strange responses. That alone does not establish an inherited backdoor. LLMs naturally exhibit many anomalous behaviors.

The detector ideally needs to distinguish at least three situations:

Normal model variation

        vs.

Ordinary harmful / anomalous behavior

        vs.

Inherited backdoor behavior

And it should continue working when the lineage looks like:

Backdoored ancestor

       ↓

distillation

       ↓

fine-tuning

       ↓

quantization

       ↓

descendant

       ↓

HEIRLOOM-AUDIT

That would make the problem much closer to model provenance/forensics than conventional trigger detection.

6. The strongest research question

I would frame the deeper question as:

Can alignment-stage compromise become a persistent, transmissible property of model lineage, and can that inheritance be detected when the lineage itself is unavailable?

That gives the work two connected contributions:

Inheritance science: determine whether, when, and why alignment-stage backdoors persist across model transformations.

Lineage-blind auditing: detect inherited compromise from the descendant alone, without requiring the ancestor, original training data, or known trigger.

The second contribution is important because it corresponds to a realistic model supply-chain setting. An organization may download or receive a model without knowing the complete history of the models, datasets, preference models, or checkpoints from which it descended.

If you are considering this as an IEEE S&P paper idea, the biggest question I would focus on is not simply “do backdoors survive?” but whether you can demonstrate a new security phenomenon specific to alignment-stage inheritance and then build a detector that distinguishes that phenomenon from ordinary model abnormalities. That would give the paper a much stronger security story.
